On 21 October 2007, Turkish voters approved constitutional amendments with approximately 69% voting yes. The package introduced direct presidential elections, replacing election by parliament, and changed the presidency from a single seven-year term to a five-year term renewable once.

The referendum followed a crisis over the governing AKP’s nomination of Abdullah Gül. Secularist opponents feared that his Islamist political background threatened the presidency’s role as a counterweight to the government. Parliamentary boycotts, a Constitutional Court quorum ruling, and military pressure had intensified the dispute. Gül was eventually elected by parliament before the referendum.

Normatively, popular election offered more direct democratic authorization and reduced opportunities for parliamentary obstruction and unelected intervention. However, electoral legitimacy alone does not guarantee liberal democracy. Giving a president an independent popular mandate within a parliamentary system risked competing claims to authority, institutional conflict, and greater personalization of power.

Overall, the reform was **democratically defensible but institutionally incomplete**: it changed presidential legitimacy without comprehensively resolving the relationship between president, government, and parliament. It did not itself create the executive presidency established through later amendments, but it made robust checks and clear constitutional boundaries more important.
