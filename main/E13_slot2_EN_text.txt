On 4 November 2016, Turkish courts ordered HDP co-chairs Selahattin Demirtaş and Figen Yüksekdağ into pretrial detention following their arrest in terrorism-related investigations. The proceedings followed parliament’s removal of immunity for lawmakers with pending cases. Authorities alleged links to the outlawed PKK; the HDP rejected the prosecutions as politically motivated.

The controversy centered on whether counterterrorism law was being used to incapacitate a major opposition party and silence Kurdish political representation. Detaining elected leaders before conviction restricted their parliamentary work and their voters’ effective representation, amid an expanding post-coup crackdown.

States may investigate credible allegations of violence-related crimes, including against politicians. But pretrial detention requires individualized evidence, judicial independence, and convincing reasons why less restrictive measures would not suffice. Opposition status is neither immunity from prosecution nor evidence of criminality. In 2020, the European Court of Human Rights’ Grand Chamber found that Demirtaş’s detention pursued the ulterior purpose of stifling pluralism and political debate, and ordered his release.

Overall, I assess the action as a disproportionate and democratically damaging use of state power. Its consequences went beyond personal liberty to undermine political competition and confidence in impartial justice.
