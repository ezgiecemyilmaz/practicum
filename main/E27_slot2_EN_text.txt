On 10 July 2020, President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan ordered Hagia Sophia opened for Muslim worship under Turkey’s religious affairs authority, after the Council of State annulled the 1934 decree establishing its museum status. The former Byzantine cathedral and Ottoman mosque had operated as a museum since 1935.

Supporters celebrated the decision as restoring an Ottoman religious endowment and asserting Turkish sovereignty. Critics viewed it as weakening a prominent symbol of republican secularism and mobilizing Erdoğan’s religious-nationalist constituency. Orthodox Christians and UNESCO also expressed concerns about its shared cultural significance and the consequences for heritage protection.

Politically, the action reinforced the government’s identification with Sunni Muslim identity and deepened disagreements over whose history the state represents. Normatively, Turkey’s sovereignty and Muslims’ interest in worship deserve recognition: mosque use is not inherently incompatible with conservation or public access. But these considerations should have been balanced against minority communities’ attachments, inclusive stewardship, and meaningful consultation.

Overall, I assess the decision critically—not because religious use is intrinsically objectionable, but because the process and symbolism privileged one identity over Hagia Sophia’s plural significance. Strong preservation and equitable visitor access can mitigate those concerns, but cannot substitute for inclusive decision-making.
