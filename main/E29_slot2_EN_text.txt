On 23 September 2021, Turkey’s central bank cut its one-week repo rate from **19% to 18%**. Annual consumer inflation was about 19.3%, far above its 5% target. The bank emphasized temporary price pressures and measures of underlying inflation; the lira weakened following the announcement.

The decision was politically controversial because President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan had repeatedly demanded lower rates and dismissed central-bank governors. It therefore intensified doubts about monetary-policy independence and whether presidential preferences were displacing the bank’s price-stability mandate. Erdoğan’s contention that high interest rates cause inflation also conflicted with mainstream monetary analysis.

Lower rates could support borrowing, investment, and employment during the pandemic recovery. However, cutting while inflation was elevated risked weakening confidence and the currency, raising import costs and worsening inflation. These risks had unequal consequences: poorer households and people holding lira savings had limited protection against lost purchasing power.

**Overall, I regard this cut as poorly justified.** Growth and employment are legitimate public goals, but pursuing them through premature easing threatened both institutional credibility and economic security. The decision warranted stronger evidence and a clearer public explanation of its inflation risks and distributional costs.
