Law No. 7346, published on 25 December 2021, amended Turkey’s vocational-training rules. It made the state cover MESEM students’ statutory minimum payments and raised remuneration for final-year students with journeyman status to at least 50% of the minimum wage; other MESEM students remained entitled to at least 30%. This improved remuneration while reducing employers’ costs, encouraging expansion of an existing model combining limited classroom instruction with substantial workplace training.

Supporters presented this as a way to develop skills, tackle youth unemployment and strengthen school–industry links. Critics, including education unions, argued that publicly subsidizing employers could turn vocational education into a supply of cheap child labor. Many participants are minors, and workplace-heavy training raises concerns about safety, supervision, educational quality and opportunities for further study.

Politically, the measure aligned vocational policy with employers’ labor needs while making taxpayers finance remuneration. Normatively, higher pay is a genuine benefit, but payment alone does not make work educational or safe. My overall assessment is therefore critical but qualified: supporting apprentices financially is defensible, yet expanding employer subsidies without commensurate safeguards risks prioritizing inexpensive labor over children’s development. Funding should be conditional on enforceable training standards, workplace inspections and meaningful educational pathways.
