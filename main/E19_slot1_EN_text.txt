On 20 March 2021, President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan announced Turkey’s withdrawal from the Council of Europe’s Istanbul Convention by presidential decision. The treaty requires measures to prevent violence against women, protect survivors, and prosecute perpetrators. Turkey, its first signatory, formally left on 1 July 2021.

The decision prompted protests by women’s organizations and opposition parties amid persistent femicide and domestic violence. Conservative supporters claimed the convention undermined traditional family values and promoted LGBTQ rights; the government maintained that domestic law provided adequate protection. Critics disputed these arguments and challenged the president’s authority to withdraw unilaterally from a treaty approved by parliament.

Politically, the action reinforced the government’s alignment with socially conservative constituencies while highlighting executive dominance and deepening polarization. Normatively, it ended Turkey’s obligations and independent monitoring under this convention, although domestic protections, including Law No. 6284, remained in force.

Overall, the withdrawal was a regressive and poorly justified policy choice. Although the treaty permits withdrawal, that possibility does not establish the democratic legitimacy or wisdom of this particular decision. Leaving weakened international accountability and signaled reduced commitment to combating gender-based violence, without demonstrating how withdrawal would improve protection for women.
