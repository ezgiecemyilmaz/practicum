On 23 February 2013, Turkey’s government authorized three parliamentarians from the pro-Kurdish Peace and Democracy Party (BDP) to meet imprisoned PKK leader Abdullah Öcalan on İmralı Island. The visit facilitated communication within the emerging peace process, aimed at ending decades of armed conflict.

The decision was controversial because the PKK is designated a terrorist organization by Turkey, the EU and the United States. Nationalist opponents viewed engagement with Öcalan as legitimizing violence and betraying its victims. Other concerns included secrecy, limited parliamentary involvement and possible concessions. Kurdish supporters welcomed dialogue, while questioning whether negotiations would deliver durable rights guarantees.

**My assessment is that authorizing the meeting was justified.** Öcalan’s influence over the PKK made engagement practically relevant to securing a ceasefire; talking to him did not itself excuse violence, grant immunity or accept his political demands. Including elected Kurdish representatives also strengthened the possibility of pursuing political rather than armed solutions.

Nevertheless, neither Öcalan nor a small delegation could represent all Kurdish perspectives. Confidential negotiations needed eventual democratic scrutiny, broader participation and safeguards for rights and victims’ interests. Overall, the authorization was a constructive, proportionate peacebuilding measure—not an endorsement of the PKK—whose promise depended on accountable follow-through.
