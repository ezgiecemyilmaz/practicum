On 23 July 2016, Turkey issued Decree Law No. 667, the first emergency decree following the failed military coup. It closed more than 2,000 schools, universities, medical facilities, associations, and other institutions accused of links to the Gülen movement, which the government blamed for the coup. It also authorized dismissals across public institutions, transferred closed institutions’ assets to state control, and extended maximum police detention to 30 days.

The controversy concerned both **scope and safeguards**. Measures could rest on broadly defined organizational connections rather than proven participation in wrongdoing. Permanent closures and dismissals, weakened detention protections, and legal protections for officials implementing the decree raised serious concerns about collective punishment, accountability, and effective remedies. Politically, the decree strengthened executive power and created opportunities to remove perceived opponents alongside genuine security threats.

The attempted coup justified urgent action to protect civilians and constitutional government. Nevertheless, emergency measures should remain necessary, proportionate, temporary where possible, and subject to independent review. **Overall, the decree was an overbroad and disproportionate response**: its legitimate security purpose did not justify sweeping, potentially irreversible sanctions without adequate individualized scrutiny and meaningful due process. Defending democracy required protecting its legal safeguards, not merely defeating its armed challengers.
