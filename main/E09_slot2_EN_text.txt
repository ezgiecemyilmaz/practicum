On 28 June 2015, Turkish police dispersed people gathering for Istanbul’s annual Pride march near Taksim Square and İstiklal Avenue, using water cannon, tear gas, and rubber bullets. Organizers said authorities had prohibited the event at the last minute because it coincided with Ramadan. The intervention broke with more than a decade of largely peaceful Pride marches.

The action was politically controversial because it restricted LGBT visibility and peaceful assembly amid concerns about the government’s increasingly conservative and authoritarian direction. Invoking religious sensitivities raised questions about whether the state protected all citizens equally or allowed majority preferences to determine minorities’ access to public space.

Normatively, authorities may impose narrowly tailored restrictions to address demonstrable safety risks, but restrictions must be necessary, proportionate, and nondiscriminatory. Ramadan or anticipated public disapproval would not, by themselves, justify suppressing a peaceful march. Police should distinguish individuals posing concrete threats from peaceful participants and prioritize de-escalation.

Overall, on the reported facts, the dispersal was a disproportionate and discriminatory restriction on fundamental freedoms. Rather than demonstrating an adequately substantiated security necessity, it stigmatized LGBT citizens and weakened confidence in impartial policing and equal citizenship.
