On 21 March 2016, Turkey’s Council of Ministers authorized emergency expropriation of roughly 82% of the parcels in Sur, Diyarbakır’s historic, predominantly Kurdish district. The decision followed prolonged curfews and destructive fighting between security forces and PKK-linked militants, which had displaced many residents. Authorities presented the measure as necessary for reconstruction, public safety and heritage restoration.

The controversy concerned not simply rebuilding, but who would control and inhabit the rebuilt district. Residents, local representatives and rights groups feared permanent displacement, inadequate compensation and exclusion from planning. The decision covered areas beyond those most damaged by fighting. Against the background of the Kurdish conflict, critics saw extensive central-government control over property as potentially enabling political and demographic restructuring. Redevelopment also raised concerns about Sur’s cultural and religious heritage.

Reconstruction was a legitimate public objective, and expropriation can sometimes serve it. However, emergency acquisition requires demonstrated necessity, proportionality, fair compensation and effective judicial review. Residents’ ability to return and participate in rebuilding was equally important.

Overall, the measure’s sweeping scope made it difficult to justify as a narrowly tailored emergency response. Targeted acquisition, transparent planning and enforceable protections against displacement would have offered a more legitimate and equitable approach.
