On 5 August 2025, Turkey’s **National Solidarity, Brotherhood and Democracy Commission** began work, bringing the renewed Kurdish peace process into Parliament. Its establishment created a cross-party forum for considering political and legal measures associated with ending the PKK conflict.

The controversy concerned both legitimacy and trust. Nationalist opponents feared that engagement would legitimize the PKK or reward armed violence with concessions. Kurdish-rights advocates and other opposition critics questioned whether a government repressing Kurdish political representation would deliver meaningful democratic reforms, rather than treating peace simply as disarmament. Transparency and the possibility of using reconciliation for governing-party advantage were additional concerns.

Politically, parliamentary involvement could broaden ownership of the process and reduce dependence on private bargains between leaders. However, executive dominance could turn the commission into a vehicle for ratifying predetermined decisions. Normatively, pursuing peace is justified, but legitimacy requires inclusive participation, equal citizenship, accountability, and recognition of victims’ suffering. Peacebuilding should neither excuse violence nor make Kurdish civil rights conditional on an armed organization’s conduct.

**Overall, establishing the commission was a constructive but insufficient step.** Its value depended on whether it enabled genuine deliberation and enforceable, rights-respecting reforms—not merely symbolic consultation or political bargaining.
